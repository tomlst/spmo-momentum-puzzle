Equity Factor Research · S&P 500 Momentum

The SPMO Momentum Puzzle

What drives the S&P 500 Momentum Index, and can its risk be controlled?

Shaotong (Tom) Li  ·  October 2026  ·  reproducible from github.com/tomlst/spmo-momentum-puzzle

Data: CRSP · Compustat · Fama-French (WRDS) Universe: S&P 500, survivorship-free In-sample: 1995–2025 Out-of-sample: 1975–1994, run once
Abstract

The academic momentum premium has faded since the late 1990s, yet the Invesco S&P 500 Momentum ETF (SPMO) compounded at 17.5% a year against 14.3% for the S&P 500 over 2015–2025. This study replicates its index with CRSP data (correlation 0.992 with SPMO) and decomposes the strategy into its three ingredients, namely momentum selection, market-cap weighting and a volatility adjustment.

In-sample, SPMO's returns are largely explained by market beta, momentum exposure and a large-cap tilt, with a positive but statistically insignificant alpha. Its edge over 2015–2025 came from holding the S&P 500's large-cap momentum winners as a group, not from concentrated single-stock bets; the volatility adjustment adds nothing. Its weakness is drawdowns (−64% in 2000–2003). Sizing the position by a HAR forecast of next month's volatility raises the in-sample Sharpe ratio from 0.51 to 0.72 and roughly halves the maximum drawdown. In a pre-registered out-of-sample test on 1975–1994 the improvement has the same sign but is smaller and not significant (Exhibit 1).

0. Motivation and research questions

SPMO has been one of the standout equity ETFs of the past two years. Measured with CRSP total returns, it gained 45.8% in 2024 and 26.6% in 2025, against 24.9% and 17.7% for SPY. Over its full history (Nov-2015 to Dec-2025) it compounded at 17.5% a year versus 14.3% (Table 1).

This is puzzling. Hwang and Rubesam (2015) find that the momentum premium in U.S. stocks largely disappeared after the late 1990s, and in this sample the Fama-French momentum factor (UMD) earned a Sharpe ratio of only 0.16 over 2015–2025 (0.30 over 1995–2014). How did a momentum ETF beat the market while the momentum factor faded?

SPMO's index is often described simply as "momentum", but it combines three ingredients. It selects stocks on momentum divided by volatility, and it weights them by market cap times a momentum score. The project asks two questions.

  • Attribution. Does the outperformance come from momentum, from the volatility adjustment, or from market-cap weighting?
  • Improvement. Given the answer, can the construction be improved?

Sample design. The in-sample period is 1995–2025, which contains SPMO's whole live history. The out-of-sample period is a held-out earlier period, 1975–1994, from the same survivorship-free universe. Periods are split by rebalance reference date, so no portfolio spans both. The out-of-sample period was evaluated once, after the design was frozen at git tag pre-oos. It overlaps the sample in which momentum was first documented (Jegadeesh and Titman, 1993) and falls in a strong-momentum regime, so it tests this project's construction choices, not the existence of momentum.

1. Data

Motivation
A fifty-year study of an index strategy is only as good as its universe, and using today's S&P 500 members would build survivorship bias into every result.
Method
  • Point-in-time universe. The universe includes every stock that was an S&P 500 member at any time since 1974, taken from CRSP's historical constituent list (1,680 securities) and applied month by month.
  • Returns. CRSP (CIZ) daily and monthly total returns are used, and monthly returns include delisting returns.
  • Links and fundamentals. CRSP–Compustat links give the primary share class priority over secondary ones, so pairs such as GOOG and GOOGL stay linked. Compustat provides book equity and GICS sectors.
  • Factors. The Fama-French five factors and momentum come from WRDS, and the short- and long-term reversal factors from the Kenneth French data library.
  • Validation. An automated script checks completeness, keys, calendars, return consistency and coverage, and fails on any structural problem.
Finding
The checks found no structural failures (Table 2). 499–506 constituents at every month-end; 99.99% of member-months have a return (the 37 gaps are missing in CRSP itself); 99.85% of member-months link to Compustat. Monthly returns equal compounded daily returns except in delisting months.

2. Replicating SPMO

Motivation
SPMO has ten years of live returns. Studying the strategy over fifty years, and switching its ingredients on and off, requires a replica built from CRSP.
Method

Following S&P's methodology (S&P Dow Jones Indices, 2026), at the end of each February and August (reference month t), the replica applies four rules.

  • Signal. The signal is x = 12-1 month total return ÷ annualised daily volatility over months t−12 to t−1 (at least 150 trading days).
  • Selection. The replica holds the top 20% of S&P 500 members by x (about 100 stocks).
  • Weights. Each stock is weighted by market cap × max(x, 0), capped at min(9%, 3 × its market-cap weight among the selected), with the excess redistributed pro rata.
  • Holding. The portfolio is bought and held for six months from t+2, with weights drifting with returns.

Ten remaining differences from the index are documented, for example total rather than float-adjusted market cap, raw x instead of S&P's winsorised z-score, and no 20% selection buffer.

Finding

The capped replica tracks SPMO with correlation 0.992 and tracking error 2.0% (Table 3), and returns 17.0% a year against SPMO's 17.5% (which is after fees).

The weight cap matters for replication accuracy, because SPMO applies it. Without it, correlation falls to 0.979 and the 2024 return jumps to 62.1%, against 45.8% for SPMO. It barely matters for long-run performance, since over 1995–2025 the uncapped and capped versions return 12.7% and 12.4% a year, with the same Sharpe ratio of 0.63.

3. Attribution with a 2 × 2 × 2 design

Motivation
Answer the first research question, namely which of the three ingredients earns the outperformance.
Method

Three switches define eight portfolios that share the same universe, rebalancing dates and holding rules; only selection and weights differ.

  • M (momentum) holds the top quintile by momentum ÷ σ and weights by momentum, instead of holding the whole S&P 500.
  • C (market cap) multiplies the weights by market cap.
  • V (volatility) divides the weights by σ.

A three-digit code gives the switches, so 000 is the equal-weighted S&P 500 and 111 is the SPMO construction. Effects are differences of monthly returns. A conditional effect switches one ingredient on with M fixed (e.g. C (market) = cap weighting within the S&P 500, C (momentum) = cap weighting within the momentum quintile); an interaction is the difference between the two, e.g. M × C = C (momentum) − C (market).

Finding

Table 4 reports the eight portfolios, and Exhibit 2 shows every effect with its confidence interval. No main effect is significant, and V contributes nothing (−0.4% a year, both in the market and among winners). The only significant effect is the M × C interaction, +2.8% a year (t = 2.84 with independent months, 2.39 Newey-West). Cap weighting loses 0.8% a year across the S&P 500 but gains 2.0% within the momentum quintile. No single pair of portfolios differs significantly on its own (Exhibit 3).

Why portfolio 111 is uncapped
The 9% cap is defined relative to market-cap weight, so it has no counterpart in the four portfolios that do not use market cap; capping 111 alone would make it differ from its neighbours in more than one ingredient. The two versions perform almost identically (12.7% vs 12.4% a year in-sample, Sharpe 0.63 for both; 16.6% vs 16.5% out of sample); the cap mainly lowers concentration (average largest weight 14.2% vs 8.5% at the 2015–2025 rebalances).

4. Is momentum × market cap real? Three diagnostics

The interaction is the only significant effect, so it deserves scrutiny. Is it a few months, a few stocks, or a factor in disguise?

4a. When did it happen?

Method
Decompose M × C by month and quarter, and break its four largest episodes into stocks and size groups.
Finding

M × C is concentrated in a few episodes, namely 2000Q4, 2009Q2, the post-COVID rebound and 2024Q1 (Exhibit 4). Excluding its two largest calendar years (+33.6% in 2009 and +29.7% in 2024), it falls to +1.4% a year (t = 1.48). Two different mechanisms produce it (Table 5).

  • Cap weighting loses in the whole market. In 2000Q4, 2009Q2 and 2020–21, small and beaten-down stocks rebounded, so C (market) was sharply negative (−9% to −16%); M × C subtracts it.
  • Cap weighting wins among winners. In 2024Q1 the largest momentum stocks rallied. NVDA, 33% of the momentum × market-cap portfolio against 3% of the momentum-weighted one, alone contributed 18.5 points.

These episodes explain the average of M × C. They do not show that the strategy's edge relies on a few stocks; that question is answered by the double sort below.

4b. Double sort with groups instead of weights

Method

Replace continuous weight tilts with groups of stocks, so no single name can dominate. At each rebalance the S&P 500 is sorted into quintiles of momentum ÷ σ, then each momentum quintile is sorted into size quintiles (a conditional sort with about 20 stocks per cell, equal-weighted). A conditional sort is used because momentum and size are correlated, and an independent sort would leave cells such as small winners almost empty; the cost is that S5 is not the same firm size in every momentum quintile. Size is measured as current market cap and, as a check, market cap twelve months earlier, before the momentum window.

Definitions
Size slope = return of the largest size quintile minus the smallest (S5 − S1) within one momentum quintile. The average slope over the five momentum quintiles is the counterpart of C (market); the slope in M5, the winners, is the counterpart of C (momentum); M5 − average is the counterpart of M × C.
Finding
  • The interaction is a broad cross-sectional effect. M5 − average = +4.9% a year (t = 2.75). Size slopes are negative in the lower and middle momentum quintiles (M1 −4.3%, M3 −5.4%) and positive among winners (+2.4%), as Exhibit 5 shows.
  • About half is past returns in disguise. With size measured before the momentum window, the interaction falls to +2.4% (t = 1.38). Exhibit 6 compares the cell returns under both size measures. Current market cap partly re-encodes the past year's return.
  • It is concentrated in 1995–2014 (+7.4%, t = 3.11) and near zero in 2015–2025 (+0.3%), because in that decade large caps beat small caps in every momentum quintile. The cap-weighted M × C was still +2.3% a year over 2015–2025; only M5 − average vanishes.
  • The key evidence on concentration. The large-cap winners cell (M5 × S5, about 20 stocks, equal-weighted) has Sharpe ratios of 0.62, 0.45 and 0.92 in 1995–2004, 2005–2014 and 2015–2025. The last matches the SPMO proxy's 0.93 without any single stock above about 5%, so SPMO's recent edge came from large-cap winners as a group.

4c. Factor regressions

Method
Regress each spread, and the proxy's excess return, on FF5 + UMD, then add the short- and long-term reversal factors; Newey-West standard errors (6 lags); full period and two subperiods.
Finding
  • C (market) is fully explained by size and value (alpha −0.1%, Table 6). Cap weighting lags equal weighting because it forgoes SMB and HML.
  • About a third of M × C is explained by profitability (RMW loading +0.18, t = 3.8); the remaining alpha is +1.9% (t = 1.78).
  • The double-sort interaction (M5 − average) keeps an alpha of +4.4% (t = 2.29), mostly from 1995–2014 (+6.7%, t = 2.81), and with lagged size it is insignificant. Adding the short- and long-term reversal factors changes no alpha by more than 0.1 points (Table 6), although some loadings on them are significant within a subperiod (Exhibit 7).
  • The SPMO proxy has no significant alpha. The alpha is +0.7% a year (t = 0.58; 95% interval roughly −1.7% to +3.1%), with market β 1.03, UMD 0.29, SMB −0.17 and R² 0.82. Its returns are largely explained by known factors; the alpha is positive but statistically indistinguishable from zero, not proven to be zero. By subperiod, its momentum loading rises to 0.38 in 2015–2025 (Exhibit 7).

Answer to question 1, in-sample
SPMO's edge is market beta + momentum exposure + a large-cap tilt, because it holds the S&P 500's large-cap momentum winners. There is no significant alpha, no reliance on a few stocks, and the volatility adjustment does nothing. The broad long-short momentum factor did fade, but large-cap winners inside the S&P 500 had their best decade in 2015–2025 (Sharpe 0.92 against 0.45 the decade before), and SPMO's rules sat squarely on that group. The price is drawdowns of −64% in 2000–2003 and −58% in 2008–2009.

5. Improvement through volatility targeting

Motivation
Momentum × market-cap weighting has no risk control, and drawdowns are its main weakness. The proxy's monthly realised volatility clusters (Exhibit 8). Its first-order autocorrelation is 0.65 (0.33 at lag 3), so this month's volatility says something about next month's.

Exhibit 8. Monthly realised volatility of the SPMO proxy, 1995–2025. High-volatility months come in clusters (2000–2002, 2008–2009, 2020, 2022).

5a. HAR volatility targeting

Method
  • Forecast. Volatility is forecast with a HAR model (Corsi, 2009) in logs, log RVm+1 = b₀ + b₁ log RV1m + b₂ log RV3m + b₃ log RV12m, where RV1, RV3 and RV12 are annualised realised volatilities over the last 1, 3 and 12 months (one month = 21 trading days), with the lognormal bias correction exp(fit + s²/2).
  • No look-ahead. The model is re-estimated every month on an expanding window of data available at the time. The 12-month input needs 12 months of history and the regression at least 24 pairs, so targeting starts in Apr-1998.
  • Sizing. Exposure is 15% ÷ forecast with no cap. The rest earns (or borrows at) the T-bill rate, and there are no costs.
Finding

The forecast's out-of-sample R² (log volatility) is 0.39, against 0.33 for last month's volatility (Exhibit 9). Targeting raises the Sharpe ratio from 0.51 to 0.72 (z = 2.55) and cuts the maximum drawdown from −64% to −35% over Apr-1998 to Dec-2025 (Table 7 and Exhibit 10). The gain comes from the two prolonged crises, when exposure fell to 0.25–0.5 for months; over 2015–2025 the two are level (0.89 vs 0.93).

The choice of target does not matter for the Sharpe ratio. With no cap and no costs, the target only scales exposure, so 10%, 15% and 20% give the same Sharpe (0.72) at different risk levels. The window does matter. Over the whole in-sample period, with fully invested warm-up years, the figures are 0.85 vs 0.63 (Exhibit 1).

5b. Do better forecasts make better portfolios?

Method
Compare HAR with three alternatives under the same sizing rule, over Apr-2000 to Dec-2025.
  • Ridge regression on ten features, namely proxy volatility over 1, 3, 6 and 12 months, the ex-ante volatility of next month's weights over the last 63 and 126 days, S&P 500 volatility over 1 and 3 months, and S&P 500 returns over 1 and 12 months. The penalty is chosen each month by rolling-origin validation over the last 24 months.
  • GARCH(1,1) on daily returns, re-estimated monthly by maximum likelihood; forecast = average variance over the next 21 days.
  • EWMA with decay 0.97 (RiskMetrics), no estimated parameters.
Finding

All four beat the unmanaged proxy significantly (Table 8), so the value of targeting does not hinge on the model. But the rankings invert. By forecast accuracy the order is ridge > GARCH > HAR > EWMA, while by Sharpe ratio it is HAR > EWMA > GARCH > ridge, and ridge is significantly worse than HAR.

Forecast accuracy is not investment value. Accuracy averages errors over all months; targeting gains come from exposure during a few prolonged crises. Slow, smooth forecasts such as HAR and EWMA stay de-risked through those episodes, while ridge, with unstable coefficients early on, re-levered into the 2001–02 decline (Exhibit 11).

6. Out-of-sample test, pre-registered

Motivation
Do the in-sample conclusions survive a different market regime?
Method
  • Freeze the design (git tag pre-oos), publish the evaluation script, then run it once.
  • Every setting is the in-sample one; HAR is estimated on out-of-sample data only (targeting starts in Apr-1978); EWMA (0.97) is carried along as the simplest alternative.
  • The benchmark is the CRSP S&P 500 value-weighted index.
Finding
  • The proxy beat the S&P 500 by 2.6 points a year (16.5% vs 13.9%, Apr-1975 to Mar-1995), with a Sharpe of 0.57 vs 0.48 (z = 0.94, not significant).
  • Targeting improved the Sharpe ratio from 0.61 to 0.70 (HAR z = 1.23, EWMA z = 1.15) and the maximum drawdown from −31% to −25%, with HAR and EWMA nearly identical (Table 9 and Exhibit 1, right).
  • October 1987 was not avoided, because exposure was about 0.9 going in (Exhibit 12). Cutting exposure to about 0.25 afterwards limited the later losses.
  • The results point in the same direction as in-sample but are smaller and not significant, because this period had no prolonged momentum crash for the overlay to sidestep.

6b. Post-OOS observations

Exploratory
The analyses in this subsection were run after the one-time out-of-sample test, with every setting unchanged. They are supplementary observations, not pre-registered tests.
  • M × C does not hold out of sample. Over 1975–1994 it is −0.9% a year (t = −1.07); over 1975–2025 as a whole, +1.4% (t = 1.67). Cap weighting lost money both across the market (−2.6%) and among winners (−3.5%) in a period when small caps led. "Momentum × market cap is the key ingredient" holds for 1995–2025 only (Exhibit 13).
  • The proxy still beat the market out of sample through momentum itself. Momentum selection earned +3.5% a year (t = 2.13) while cap weighting cost 3.0% (t = −3.22). UMD averaged +10.2% a year in that period, and the proxy's UMD loading of 0.45 accounts for 4.6 points a year of its excess over the S&P 500, with an alpha of −0.8% (not significant). An equal-weighted momentum portfolio (100) returned 20.8% a year against 16.5% for the proxy.
  • The puzzle, refined. The same rules beat the market in both eras for different reasons. In 1975–1994 the edge came through the momentum factor, with cap weighting a drag. In 2015–2025 it came through large-cap winners, with the momentum factor weak.
  • Fifty-year view. With HAR estimated on one expanding window from 1975, $1 in Apr-1978 grows to $1,235 with targeting, $615 for the proxy and $274 for the S&P 500 (Sharpe 0.77, 0.62 and 0.58; drawdown −40%, −64%, −51%). Nearly all of the improvement comes from 2000–2002 and 2008–2009; in the 2020s targeting lagged (0.77 vs 0.92) because sudden shocks were followed by fast rebounds (Exhibit 14).

7. Conclusions

  • SPMO has no hidden alpha. It beat the S&P 500 by holding large-cap momentum winners in 2015–2025 and through the momentum factor itself in 1975–1994. Its returns are largely explained by known factors and do not rely on a few stocks.
  • The volatility adjustment adds nothing measurable, and momentum × market cap depends on the period. The volatility adjustment changed annual returns by −0.4% in-sample and −0.3% out of sample, neither significant. Momentum × market cap added +2.8% a year in 1995–2025 but −0.9% (not significant) in 1975–1994, and +1.4% (t = 1.67) over the whole fifty years.
  • Volatility targeting is insurance against prolonged crashes. It roughly halved drawdowns in-sample and cut them out of sample; its Sharpe improvement depends on whether such crashes occur, so it is a risk-management tool, not a stable source of alpha.
  • Methodological lessons. Forecast accuracy and portfolio value diverged across four models, and simple, slow forecasts were the most robust.

8. Limitations

  • No costs. Returns are before fees and trading costs. Volatility targeting reaches 2.2× exposure (2.4× for EWMA) and borrows at the T-bill rate; real financing costs and a leverage cap would reduce its gains.
  • Daily data only. Realised volatility is built from daily, not intraday, returns, so it is a noisy input to every forecast.
  • Many tests. Several effects, specifications and subperiods were examined. Some t-stats assume independent months (the attribution and double sort); the regressions and later tests use Newey-West standard errors. Each figure states which.
  • Conditional double sort. S5 does not correspond to the same firm size in every momentum quintile.
  • Out-of-sample regime. 1975–1994 is a strong-momentum period that overlaps the original momentum literature, and it contains no prolonged momentum crash.
  • Replica, not the index. Ten documented differences remain, including total rather than float-adjusted market cap and no selection buffer.
  • Post-OOS analyses (Section 6b) are exploratory.

A. Appendix

Tried, not adopted. The study also explored alternative weighting schemes within the momentum quintile (momentum squared, rank-based weights, lagged market cap, tighter weight caps and S&P's own score), a barbell of large winners and small caps, and volatility forecasts estimated on daily samples. The daily-sample forecasts did not improve volatility targeting. The weighting schemes and the barbell looked better in some periods, but they were selected after inspecting in-sample results, so their in-sample performance is not reliable evidence, and they were not adopted.
